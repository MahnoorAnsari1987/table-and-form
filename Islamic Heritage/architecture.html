Form, light, and order

Islamic Architecture

Mosques, styles, and the grammar of sacred space

A shared visual language

Islamic architecture is not a single style. Hypostyle halls in the Maghrib, four-iwan courts in Iran, Ottoman central domes, and Mughal charbagh gardens all solved local climates and materials while circulating ideas along trade and pilgrimage routes.

Domes

The dome can mark the prayer hall's heart, symbolize the vault of heaven, and solve a structural span. Ottoman architects refined cascading semi-domes; Timurid and Persian builders wrapped them in tile. The Dome of the Rock (completed 691-692 CE) is an early masterpiece of the type, even though it is a shrine rather than a hypostyle mosque.

Minarets

From square Maghribi towers to slender Ottoman pencils and spiral experiments such as Samarra's Malwiya, the minaret became the vertical signature of the mosque. Its practical role is the call to prayer (adhan); its visual role is wayfinding in the city.

Arches

Horseshoe arches in Andalusia and the Maghrib, pointed arches in later eastern traditions, and multifoil experiments all frame light and procession. Repeating arches turn a hall into a forest of columns - as at Cordoba - inviting movement and recitation rather than a single focal statue.

Geometric patterns

Girih, zellij, and star-and-polygon compositions suggest infinity without depicting living beings. Craftspeople used compass and straightedge (and later more complex methods) to cover walls, ceilings, and floors with ordered beauty.

Calligraphy

Kufic on early monuments, later Thuluth and Naskh on Ottoman tiles and manuscripts: writing the Qur'an and sacred phrases turned buildings into readable space. Calligraphy is often called the highest of the Islamic visual arts.

Historical architecture cards

Ottoman

Imperial Friday mosques

Complexes (kulliye) combined mosque, school, kitchen, and baths. Sinan's 16th-century works set a classical standard of balanced light and structure.

Mughal

The Taj Mahal

Completed in the 17th century in Agra, it unites Persian garden planning, white marble inlay, and a monumental central dome. It is a mausoleum, not a mosque, yet it is a summit of Mughal design.

Nasrid

The Alhambra

Palace-city of Granada: muqarnas, stucco calligraphy, water courts, and gardens. A late flowering of Andalusi art before 1492.

Maghrib

Hassan II Mosque

Casablanca's oceanfront mosque (1990s) revives Moroccan craft - zellij, carved cedar, and a soaring minaret - at a contemporary civic scale.

Contemporary

Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque

Abu Dhabi's white marble campus gathers floral inlay, vast courtyards, and reflecting pools - a 21st-century statement of hospitality and craft.

Mughal

Badshahi Mosque, Lahore

A late-17th-century congregational mosque of red sandstone and marble, among the largest historic mosques of the Mughal world, with a vast courtyard for Friday prayer.
